# Temporary transit integration game

This is an original, local, synthetic test project created for GDM-002. Open `project.godot` in Godot and run the main scene. An NPC walks onto a moving ferry, rides to a station and exits; the status label shows the game phase. The game owns its NPC controller, scene, physics and vehicle movement. The copied transit add-on only coordinates requests and seat state.

Run `python run_checks.py <godot-console-executable>` for the full current 39-case suite. It includes normal travel, passenger blocking and recovery, crowd avoidance, multi-vehicle junction dispatch, full reconstruction at two crossing/recovery points, two physical riders sharing a capacity-two vehicle, and a navigation-adapter timeout when a platform is on a disconnected navigation region. The save fixtures capture coordinator and selected game-owned controller/platform state; fixed station geometry is recreated from the scene. Many routes and vehicle returns remain scripted, and the avoidance policies are bounded examples. This sample is not an existing third-party game or a released product. The copied add-on is for local testing only and remains subject to the project's release and rights review.

Three further checks cover a simple cooperative avoidance policy, a request arriving while the ferry is occupied, and a return triggered only when a new passenger requests service. The avoidance policy makes an idle passenger try a short sideways step on collision; it is game-side example logic, not general crowd navigation or a GDM-002 add-on feature. The dispatch example uses a fixed route, known schedule and straight-line return, and does not solve general vehicle routing.

Four additional checks exercise selecting the other side when the preferred side is blocked, waiting when both sides are blocked, refusing to step to an unsupported endpoint, and serving two distinct destinations with one ferry on a known timetable. The two-route dispatch test uses two scheduled services and scripted physical movement.

The opt-in extended avoidance example tries up to eight short directions, samples floor support at four points, and stops a yield if an obstacle appears immediately ahead. Four further tests cover an additional direction when both sides are blocked, a gap between supported endpoints, an obstacle appearing during a yield, and seeded multi-vehicle dispatch with varied passenger arrival times and destinations. The seeded dispatch test uses immediate simulated movement acknowledgments, not physical vehicles. Sampling can miss small gaps, and the policy does not predict future obstacle motion or establish general crowd navigation; the scheduled dispatch fixture does not establish arbitrary routing.

Two further tests exercise two passengers boarding, riding, and exiting two collision platforms at the same time, and a walking passenger who encounters two stationary passengers in sequence and triggers each to yield. The two vehicles follow scripted parallel tracks, and the riders do not collide with one another; the three-person avoidance test uses a wide flat floor and stationary blockers. These are bounded integration examples, not general traffic, vehicle dispatch or crowd-avoidance proofs.

The parallel-vehicle disruption variant disables one service while both passengers are aboard, holds that passenger's seat for physical recovery, and lets the other service reach its destination. The vehicles still follow scripted tracks and motion; no general replanning or collision avoidance between vehicles is modeled.

The moving-crowd fixture adds two head-on walkers with stable movement priorities. The lower-priority walker sidesteps, waits for the other to clear, then resumes its original request. This is bounded game-owned controller logic tested on a flat floor with two agents; it does not provide arbitrary crowd pathfinding, fairness for dynamic arrivals, or general deadlock recovery, and it is not a GDM-002 add-on feature.

The mapped physical dispatch fixture starts each vehicle from a `departed` coordinator event, selects a configured route endpoint by service, and moves two platforms along different paths at different speeds. Riders exit onto distinct station geometry and acknowledge only after reaching their mapped stops. This remains a small, preconfigured route map with two vehicles; dynamic route search, vehicle-to-vehicle collision avoidance, and schedule replanning are not covered.

A cancellation regression stops a passenger during a crowd sidestep, checks that the canceled route does not emit arrival or resume moving, and confirms the other passenger can still finish. This covers one two-agent cancellation case, not general crowd fairness or recovery.

The three- and four-walker fixtures send moving passengers through intersecting routes and check each arrival signal against its own intended endpoint. A four-way crossing exposed an indefinite wait when another passenger remained nearby; the game-owned controller now retries its original route after a bounded wait. This is a basic progress rule, not a proof of fairness or general deadlock freedom. A mapped-dispatch disruption fixture disables one service during physical travel, returns that platform and passenger to an allowed safe dock, then confirms the other vehicle continues to its destination. The recovered passenger boards the same vehicle's separately scheduled alternate service and completes the trip. These remain bounded, preconfigured examples; they do not establish arbitrary multi-agent pathfinding, inter-vehicle collision avoidance or dynamic timetable replanning.

The crossing-vehicle fixtures give platforms perpendicular routes through shared junctions. A fixed priority reserves each junction while other platforms wait; tests check that collision bounds do not overlap and all routes eventually arrive. The multi-junction case uses three vehicles and two known intersections. These fixtures do not establish arbitrary geometry, deadlock-free scheduling or a GDM-002 add-on feature.

An interruption case changes a vehicle's route while it holds a shared-junction reservation. It rejects a recovery point inside the junction, returns the vehicle to a configured safe point outside it, then confirms the waiting vehicle proceeds after the reservation clears. This is a direct game-owned physical movement test and does not model coordinator schedules, passenger state, arbitrary safe-point selection or route replanning.

A coordinator-driven crossing case starts two physical routes only from actual `departed` events. Disabling one service while its vehicle holds the junction makes the coordinator request recovery; the vehicle retreats to the service's safe stop, releases the reservation, and the waiting vehicle proceeds and physically alights its passenger. Boarding and alighting acknowledgments in this fixture are test-driven coordinator calls with rider marker nodes following the platforms; they do not exercise the controller bridge or walking passengers. Route geometry and safe-stop mapping remain fixed, and this single interruption does not prove general traffic fairness or deadlock freedom.

Six deterministic crossing layouts translate the shared intersection and rotate the perpendicular paths, with route priority alternated between scenarios. They check vehicle bounds, arrival and endpoints. One exact quarter-turn case did not record a wait for the configured lower-priority vehicle, so the generated-layout test does not assert a wait ordering; fixed cardinal tests separately verify that a crossing vehicle waits. The fixtures remain direct single-junction routes and do not prove general route planning or conflict resolution.

An additional seeded physical-dispatch fixture builds eight reproducible perpendicular crossings with varied translated centers, crossing angles, platform speeds and priorities. Each run checks that both physical platforms avoid overlap and reach their assigned endpoints. This broadens regression coverage of the reservation controller over generated single-junction pairs; it still does not cover multi-junction route search, dynamic route changes, fairness guarantees, deadlock freedom or arbitrary geometry.

An eight-seed generated-fleet case dispatches three physical platforms on 60-degree-spaced routes through one shared junction. Launches are staggered and speed, priority, center and base angle vary. The fixture sets a 4-unit reservation radius for its acute crossings and 2-unit square footprints. At the dispatcher's 3-unit default, one sampled layout produced overlapping bounds; using 4 units avoided overlap in all eight tested layouts. Raising the default globally caused existing routes ending at radius 4 to retain junction ownership and block another vehicle, so the safer policy depends on endpoint geometry and needs a per-scene clearance rule. This bounded test does not establish general collision avoidance.

A reconstruction case saves coordinator state, a versioned dispatcher snapshot, and platform positions while one vehicle is inside a reserved junction and another is waiting. It destroys and recreates the small synthetic scene, registers fresh platform bodies, restores dispatcher routes/priority/progress, then disables one service and verifies safe-stop recovery, continued crossing, physical bounds and seat release through the controller bridge. Unsupported dispatcher snapshot versions are rejected without mutating the registered routes. This fixture does not establish full game-world persistence or route migration between changed scene geometry.

The reconstructed crossing now uses CharacterBody passengers rather than marker nodes. They walk from dock surfaces onto the platforms, are attached to their moving vehicles while aboard, and walk onto the safe-stop or destination floor before the bridge accepts recovery or alighting. Both a mid-crossing save and a second save during safe-stop travel recreate the coordinator, bridge, dispatcher, platforms and riders; the pending recovery is republished after restore. Wrong snapshot versions, changed junction coordinates, a short route and a negative wait counter are rejected without mutating the already registered dispatcher state. This is one synthetic scene and fixed boarding-pad geometry, not general passenger-world persistence or platform boarding physics for arbitrary game layouts.

A fleet reconstruction case snapshots three vehicles while one is crossing and two are waiting, rebuilds fresh vehicle bodies and dispatcher state with deliberately changed route, speed and priority values, then verifies the snapshot restores the original settings and all routes finish without overlapping bounds. It also rejects changed junction coordinates, changed vehicle membership, non-positive speed and malformed priority without mutating restored state. This tests rejection rather than migration to changed maps, and does not cover controller state outside the dispatcher snapshot or general crash recovery.

A recovery reconstruction case rejects an unsafe stop inside the junction without mutating the active route, interrupts an in-flight vehicle to a safe stop, saves partway through recovery, rebuilds the dispatcher and platform, then restores the saved recovery route and motion settings. It checks the recovery signal fires once at the safe stop and that normal arrival is not emitted. This remains a one-vehicle synthetic recovery path, not general interrupted service recovery.

A parallel recovery reconstruction saves while one vehicle is retreating from the shared junction and a second active vehicle waits. It rebuilds both vehicles, rejects a malformed wait counter without changing either registration, restores both routes and progress, checks they do not overlap, then verifies the retreat signal occurs and the waiting vehicle crosses to its endpoint. This covers one fixed perpendicular crossing and safe-stop arrangement, not general simultaneous incident handling.
